## Lead Story: More Accurate GenAI Doesn't Always Reduce Total Review Costs

**Source:** eDiscovery Today (Aug 5, 2026)

Jeremy Pickens of Elevate has published a whitepaper that challenges a core assumption in AI-powered review: that higher accuracy automatically means lower costs. The reality is more complicated.

### Key Findings

**The baseline problem:** A law firm report celebrated GenAI saving 50% versus linear review. But linear review "is a dreadfully weak baseline that is trivial to beat." An older TAR 1.0 approach would have saved nearly 70% on the same matter.

**Technology cost asymmetry:** Traditional TAR pricing is $0/document for machine classification. GenAI incurs a cost for every document analyzed (assumed $0.10/doc in the sensitivity analysis).

**Richness matters:** Lower richness (fewer responsive documents in the corpus) shrinks the review-cost component across all methods, muting precision's advantage and letting cheaper approaches catch up. Higher richness matters more for precision gains.

**Six scenarios tested:** The paper models linear review, supervised TAR 1.0, TAR 2.0, and GenAI across matter sizes from 10,000 to 5 million documents, with richness at 20% and 5%.

### The Takeaway

> "No approach is universally best across all matters. The most effective document review strategies are not driven by technology preferences, but by data, economics, and expertise."

The industry marketing says GenAI is the only way forward. The numbers say: run your own sensitivity analysis.

## Schulte v. LinkedIn: First Federal Decision Accepting GenAI for Final Responsiveness Calls

**Source:** ComplexDiscovery (July 2026, commentary resurfacing)

Judge Laurel Beeler's June 30, 2026 order in Schulte v. LinkedIn Corporation (N.D. Cal.) denied all three plaintiff demands aimed at Relativity aiR and treated the tool as technology-assisted review under the existing ESI protocol.

### What Happened

**May 15 disclosure:** LinkedIn disclosed 25 search strings and intent to use Relativity aiR under the TAR provision of the ESI order.

**May 23 clarification:** No seed or training set used; aiR makes final responsiveness calls; humans review samples from each category for quality control. Target population: 204,444 documents.

**Three demands denied:**
1. **Bar search-string culling** — Court held culling before machine review satisfies Rule 26(b) reasonableness and proportionality, citing In re Biomet (2013) and Livingston v. Chicago (2020). Ordered meet-and-confer on strings within 21 days.

2. **Run aiR across full custodial files** — Two custodians alone held 800GB; full 19-custodian set would run to multiple terabytes. Court found forcing full-scope review disproportionate.

3. **Compel validation metrics (elusion, error rates, reviewer counts)** — ESI order required disclosure of TAR use only. Demands amounted to discovery on discovery, disfavored absent specific production deficiency (citing Taylor v. Google, 2024).

### Why It Matters

**Old rules, new tools:** Beeler applied the same TAR-era authorities (In re Biomet, Livingston, Taylor v. Google) without needing the Da Silva Moore trilogy. The framework held.

**First federal acceptance of GenAI for final calls:** Retired Judge Andrew Peck and Reema Holz (DLA Piper) frame Schulte as the first federal decision to accept generative AI making the final responsiveness call.

**Asymmetry remains:** Elusion can only be measured with data the producing party holds. Requesting parties must show a specific production deficiency before compelling validation metrics.

**UK contrast:** Gormsen v. Meta Platforms (UK Competition Appeal Tribunal, Dec 2025) accepted AI as a helper under human review but stopped short of final-call authority.

### The Takeaway

The rules didn't change. The expectations moved. Custodial volume drove proportionality. Disclosure obligations began and ended with the protocol. Validation metrics weren't compelled absent deficiency.

## Source Discipline: When "Today's Legal News" Can't Be Verified

**Source:** Legal Tech Monitor (Aug 5, 2026)

Legal Tech Monitor published a sharp essay on the difficulty of verifying what counts as "today's" top legal news in a fragmented, access-restricted media environment.

### The Problem

Several significant U.S. legal stories were circulating, including a major federal-court ruling criticizing an IRS immunity settlement. But accessible sources did not support high-confidence conclusions that those items were the most important stories newly reported on August 3, 2026.

### Why It Matters for Litigators

**Timing and source quality are not editorial niceties.** Litigators need to know whether a development is genuinely new, merely recirculated, or based on an older order suddenly drawing attention.

**Mischaracterized "breaking" stories can distort:** case assessment, internal reporting, board communications, and media response strategy.

### The Standard

> "If a story cannot be tied confidently to a dated opinion, docket entry, or authoritative report, professionals should resist treating it as a same-day development."

Check whether the underlying court action actually occurred that day. Check whether a publication is resurfacing older material. Check whether multiple reliable sources align on basic facts.

### The Takeaway

For a profession built on accurate characterization of procedural posture, dates, and holdings, saying "this could not be verified confidently" is not a limitation. It's good legal judgment.
