## Lead Story: Court Equates GenAI Review with TAR

Cristin Traylor's analysis of *Schulte v. LinkedIn Corp.* (N.D. Cal. July 1, 2026) reveals what many overlooked in the ruling.

### What the Court Actually Said

Judge Beeler found that LinkedIn's use of Relativity aiR "is a form of Technology Assisted Review"—explicitly equating GenAI review with traditional TAR workflows under the ESI Order.

Key points from the ruling:
- LinkedIn disclosed on May 15, 2026 that it would use Relativity aiR to filter non-responsive documents
- This disclosure "more than satisfy[ies] the demands of the Interim ESI Order"
- The use of search strings to create target population before aiR is "not a new practice"
- Courts have consistently found keyword pre-culling satisfies Rules 26(b) and 34(b)(2) reasonableness standards

### What Was NOT Challenged

Traylor emphasizes:

> "What the plaintiffs did not do—and what neither party nor the court treated as remotely controversial—was challenge LinkedIn's use of aiR to make final responsiveness calls."

Neither the parties nor the court questioned whether GenAI can make responsiveness decisions. The disputes were operational:
1. Should search strings pre-cull before aiR processing?
2. What metrics should be disclosed about aiR performance?

### The Significance

This matters because many define TAR narrowly as traditional workflows (TAR 1.0, TAR 2.0/CAL). Judge Beeler's ruling extends TAR to include GenAI review—placing generative AI under established procedural frameworks.

Traylor's assessment:

> "Generative AI for legal data review is actively in use, and the simple fact of that use is uncontested. The questions courts are being asked to resolve are operational—and they are the same questions practitioners have been answering about TAR for more than a decade."

### Practical Takeaways

1. **Disclose AI use**: The ESI Order required disclosure of TAR intent—aiR qualifies
2. **Document workflow**: Pre-culling with search strings before AI review is accepted practice
3. **Expect operational disputes**: Metrics, precision, recall—same questions as traditional TAR
4. **Don't challenge fundamentals**: Courts are not asking whether GenAI can review; they're asking how

## Pro Se Filings: The AI-Fueled Defense Cost Center

The story from July 27 continues: federal civil pro se plaintiff rates rose from 11.33% to 16.94% after widespread generative AI access—roughly 157,080 more filings.

### The Mechanism

- AI tools draft complaints, motions, discovery requests in seconds
- Self-represented litigants submit sophisticated-looking documents without legal training
- No verification standards, no duty-of-candor training, no professional oversight
- Result: filings that look credible but may be weak, inaccurate, or fabricated

### Defense Cost Implications

Insurers and legal operations face:
- Longer pleadings requiring more review time
- Citations that must be checked for hallucinations
- More court appearances for cases that still get dismissed early
- "1,018 AI hallucinations case filings" from pro se parties as of early July

### The Gap in Commentary

Doug Austin's observation:

> "We spend so much time talking about lawyers' misuse of AI, but not near enough about the increased filings—and corresponding misuse of AI—by pro se parties. This is an even bigger challenge in the court system."
