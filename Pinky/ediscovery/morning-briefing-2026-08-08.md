## Cyber Law Toolkit Scenario 36: When Hacktivists Join the Fight

**Source:** ComplexDiscovery (Aug 8, 2026)

Scenario 36 of the Cyber Law Toolkit answers a sharp question: What does international humanitarian law demand of civilians who join a war from their keyboards?

### The Hypothetical

A volunteer hacker collective in State A runs three operations against enemy State B:

1. **DDoS on civil registry** — Knocks the e-portal offline for 10 days, but causes no permanent damage
2. **Wiper attack** — Destroys civilian records, backups, and request-processing software permanently
3. **Phone hacking** — Spear-phishes soldiers, installs spyware, provides location data to frontline units for targeting

### The Legal Framework

The toolkit is supported by NATO CCDCOE, ICRC, U.S. Naval War College, University of Exeter, Wuhan University, and Czech NCISA. It maps where states agree and split on how IHL applies in cyberspace.

**Key distinction:** DDoS that causes temporary unavailability is not an "attack" under IHL. Wiper that permanently destroys data and functionality IS an attack — under the "functionality view" that Austria, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, New Zealand, and the ICRC support.

### What Hacktivists Lose

Civilians are protected against attack "unless and for such time as they take a direct part in hostilities."

- **Wiper deployers** — Likely satisfy all three criteria for direct participation (threshold of harm, direct causation, belligerent nexus) and may be lawfully attacked
- **DDoS participants** — Likely don't reach the threshold of harm and keep protection
- **Phone hackers providing targeting data** — Likely cross into direct participation

### Criminal Stakes

The ICC's Office of the Prosecutor published its first policy on cyber-enabled crimes in December 2025:

> "Any person may be the perpetrator of a cyber-enabled war crime, irrespective of whether they act on behalf of a party to the armed conflict."

### The Governance Question

> "When a volunteer with a laptop can become a lawful military target, the governance question stops being abstract: does your organization know where support for a cause ends and participation in a conflict begins?"

Acceptable-use policies should say plainly: employees who volunteer for a combatant's cyber campaign risk losing protection from attack and face criminal exposure.

## Beijing Contests House Salt Typhoon Report

**Source:** ComplexDiscovery (Aug 8, 2026)

A bipartisan House report found three Chinese state-owned carriers — China Telecom, China Mobile, China Unicom — still holding infrastructure inside U.S. networks years after their Section 214 authorizations were denied or revoked. Within a day, Beijing pushed back.

### What the Committee Found

**Evidence stack:**
- Subpoenaed corporate records
- 8 sworn interviews
- Routing data showing China Mobile International in active paths to Salt Typhoon servers 192+ times
- 108,891 BGP hijack events (Jan 2018–May 2025) involving China/Hong Kong networks announcing U.S. address space without authorization

**Infrastructure remaining:**
- China Telecom: 10 active points of presence in 7 metro areas; ~25% Huawei transmission hardware
- China Mobile: 39 PoP entries in 27 facilities; 143+ active network assets
- China Unicom: Equipment and connections in ~10 U.S. data centers

### Beijing's Rebuttal

China's embassy and Global Times dismissed the findings as politicized and technically unsupported. State analysts argued the report ties carrier connections to Salt Typhoon "without demonstrating a causal relationship."

### Why It Matters for Insurance and Litigation

War and state-action exclusions turn on whether conduct is attributable to a sovereign. Official Chinese objections don't defeat attribution — but they keep the question contested in coverage disputes.

**The practical moves:**
- Review war-exclusion and state-action language at renewal
- Assume incident narratives will be read against contested attribution in discovery
- Preserve attribution evidence chain as a record set

### Rip-and-Replace Would Generate Records

The committee's six recommendations would create new record sets:
- Codify FCC authority over Chinese carrier infrastructure
- Fund expanded rip-and-replace
- Require logging, monitoring, and record-preservation obligations

Every measure generates procurement files, replacement schedules, and audit trails that downstream litigation could demand.
