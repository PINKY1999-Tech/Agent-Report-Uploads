## Lead Story: Obviously Relevant Documents Must Be Collected

Washington Magistrate Judge Michelle L. Peterson granted plaintiff's request to add custodians in *Wilson Aerospace LLC v. Boeing Co.*, ruling that parties cannot ignore relevant documents from non-disclosed custodians.

### What Happened

- Plaintiff sought to add ten ESI custodians, increasing total from twenty to thirty
- Defendant admitted it didn't search Patrick Murphy's documents because he wasn't a disclosed custodian
- Judge Peterson allowed "targeted expansion" adding three custodians: Gregory Ffolkes, Ivan Bush, and Patrick Murphy

### The Key Holding

> "A party therefore cannot justify failing to collect obviously relevant documents on the ground that the relevant employee was not designated as an 'ESI custodian' or that such documents might fall outside an agreed search-term workflow."

### The ESI Protocol Language

The parties' own protocol provided:
1. Discovery requests govern scope of documents to be produced
2. Search terms do not supplant discovery requests
3. Parties must conduct "reasonably diligent search of relevant custodians and shared platforms"
4. The protocol is "a mechanism to implement the Rules, not a device to contract around them"

### The Ruling's Logic

Judge Peterson stated:

> "Defendant's discovery duties are defined first and foremost by the Federal Rules and the scope of the discovery requests; the ESI Protocol cannot be invoked to avoid collecting and reviewing responsive documents from plainly relevant employees within Defendant's control."

### Practical Takeaways

1. **Custodian lists aren't limits**: Relevance drives collection obligations, not designation
2. **Search terms don't supplant requests**: Discovery scope comes from requests, not keyword lists
3. **Protocol is implementation, not contraction**: ESI protocols implement Rule 34, don't narrow it
4. **"Obviously relevant" triggers duty**: Courts won't accept avoidance arguments for plainly relevant employees

## IBM 2026 Cost of a Data Breach Report

IBM released its 2026 Cost of a Data Breach Report, showing breach costs reaching record highs.

### Key Statistics

- **Global average breach cost**: $4.99M (12% increase over last year, record high)
- **US average breach cost**: $11.5M (11% increase, more than double global average)
- **AI-driven attacks**: 56% increase, led by AI deepfake impersonations and AI-enabled malware
- **AI model inversion attack cost**: $6M average
- **AI security savings**: Organizations using AI/automation save $1.93M compared to non-users
- **AI access controls lacking**: 92% of AI-related breaches involved organizations without proper AI access controls
- **Ransomware incidents**: Rose to 39% (from 34%)

- **SOC AI agents**: 50% of organizations deploying AI agents in security operations center

### The AI Dual-Use

> "Good guys need AI since the bad guys are using it."

### Most Targeted Sectors

### The Detection Timeline

## Legal Rates: Rising, Flattening, and Falling at Once

LegalVIEW Insights Volume 2026-2 shows legal rate growth stopped behaving like one number.

### The Divergent Data

All from the same three months of invoice data:
- **Corporate work**: Up 9.9% (blended $936/hr)
- **San Francisco**: Up 17.3%
- **Am Law 151-200 associate rates**: Down 10.2% (from $434 to $390/hr)

### The Report's Conclusion

Broad market averages are becoming less useful as standalone benchmarks. Industry, firm tier, practice area, and geography now shape legal costs enough that comparison requires more precision than most departments apply.

### Where Rates Climbed

- Manufacturing: 12.2% increase (highest third-quartile partner rate at $1,340)
- Consumer services: 11.5% increase (displaced financial as most expensive sector)
- Health care: Tracking 11.1% increase
- Technology/telecom: 9.5% increase

### Where Rates Declined

- Regulatory/compliance: Slipped 0.9%
- Finance/securities: Dipped from $837 to $826
- Am Law 151-200 associates: Down 10.2%
- General drafting/review (AI-adjacent): Down 2.1%

### The AI Pricing Effect

The report offers three candidate causes for associate decline:
1. Turnover at lower-tier firms
2. Client bargaining
3. Early pricing effect from generative AI on standardized associate work

### Why Benchmarks Disagree

Three vendor datasets give different answers:
- **LegalVIEW**: Tracks average billed rates by Am Law revenue rank
- **Thomson Reuters**: Tracks worked rates by firm segment
- **CounselLink**: Groups firms by headcount band

Legal operations teams should not read these as a single verdict—they're measuring different things.
